Case study 01 Fintech · Crypto

Crypto confidence: rethinking the first purchase

First-time crypto buyers on Revolut often buy during a hype spike, panic when a normal price swing follows, and sell at a loss — then avoid the crypto product entirely. I designed a lightweight intervention to fix the moment before the panic starts.

Problem

A normal market move feels like a crisis to someone who's never seen one before

Crypto volatility isn't unusual — 2025 alone saw bitcoin swing from above $125,000 to a peak-to-trough drop of over $40,000. Experienced holders read that as Tuesday. Someone who bought their first £50 of crypto three days earlier reads it as evidence they made a mistake.

Behavioural data from the 2025–26 cycle shows the pattern clearly: buying activity peaked right near market tops, and selling activity peaked right near the bottoms — the textbook shape of panic-driven, not strategy-driven, decisions. For a first-time buyer with no context for what "normal" looks like, that's almost the default outcome.

The cost to Revolut isn't one bad trade — it's a user who associates the entire crypto product with losing money, and never opens that tab again.

Research

Where the evidence came from

Market behaviour

2025–26 crash cycles show buy activity clustering at price tops and sell activity clustering at price bottoms — a documented pattern across multiple market analyses, not a one-off event.

Review mining

Reading through app store and community reviews mentioning Revolut's crypto product surfaces a recurring theme: confusion at the first significant price drop, and a sense of having been misled about risk.

Comparable products

Robinhood and Coinbase both gate first crypto purchases behind risk-disclosure screens — but static disclaimers get skipped, not absorbed. The gap isn't information, it's context at the moment it's needed.

Solution

"Risk check" — a 30-second interstitial, shown once

Before a user's first-ever crypto purchase, they see a short flow: a chart of past volatility for the asset they're buying, a prompt to set their own "this would worry me" threshold, and confirmation that the app will use that threshold to talk to them later — not the price.

When the price later moves past that threshold, instead of silence (or nothing at all), the user gets a message that reframes the drop against their own stated expectations rather than a generic push notification.

Case study 02 Retail · Consumer

Substitutions people actually want

Nearly a third of Sainsbury's online shoppers receive a substitution they didn't choose. I work the floor where those substitutions get picked, and I know exactly where the process breaks down before the customer ever sees the swap.

Problem

Pickers guess. Customers get surprised. Nobody wins.

Industry-wide, published UK consumer research shows around 28–32% of Sainsbury's online orders include at least one substitution, and separate academic research into online grocery substitutions found 30–45% of shoppers are dissatisfied with the swap they received.

Having worked the operational side of this myself, the reason isn't a lack of care — it's a lack of information at the moment it matters. A picker finds an item out of stock mid-shop, with seconds to decide, no visibility into what that specific customer would actually accept, and no easy way to check.

The result: a customer gets a swap that technically fills the gap but breaks trust in the app's judgement — and next time, they're more likely to shop the substitution-free way, in person, somewhere else.

Research

Where the evidence came from

Frontline observation

Direct experience of the picking and substitution process from the retail floor — where the time pressure and information gap actually sit.

Published consumer data

UK-wide surveys and academic studies on grocery substitution consistently show dissatisfaction concentrated in a few root causes: mismatched brand, mismatched flavour, and items the shopper has never bought before.

Existing safeguard

Customers can already decline a substitute at the door — but that's a rejection after the fact, not a preference captured before the problem happens.

Solution

Substitution preferences, set once, used automatically

At checkout — or any time in account settings — a customer sets standing rules per category or per item: "same brand only", "any flavour is fine", "never dairy alternatives", "always ask first". Those rules travel with the order into the picker's handheld device, so the decision is already made before the shelf is empty.

For anything the rules don't cover, the picker still chooses — but the app now knows enough to flag low-confidence swaps for a quick customer confirmation via notification, instead of guessing blind.
